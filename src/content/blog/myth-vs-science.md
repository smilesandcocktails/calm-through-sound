Wellness has a hype problem, and sound therapy is not immune to it. Browse online and you'll find everything from gentle, sensible claims to promises that a particular note can "cure" disease or "detox your cells." As a practitioner who happens to value evidence, I think the honest path is also the most reassuring one, so let's sort the signal from the noise.

## The short version

<div class="vs">
<div class="col yes">
<h3>What the evidence supports</h3>
<ul>
<li>Helping the body relax and lowering stress arousal</li>
<li>Reducing tension, anxiety and low mood in the short term</li>
<li>Easing pain, fatigue and anxiety as a <em>complement</em> during treatment</li>
<li>Supporting better subjective sleep quality</li>
<li>Being low-risk, with no adverse effects reported in reviews</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div class="col no">
<h3>What it can't do (despite the claims)</h3>
<ul>
<li>Cure or treat illness, infection or disease</li>
<li>"Detox" the body or "repair DNA"</li>
<li>Replace medical, psychological or psychiatric care</li>
<li>Deliver guaranteed results from one specific frequency</li>
<li>Work the same way for everyone, every time</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>

## Where the real evidence stands

The genuinely supportive findings are consistent and unglamorous. A 2020 meta-analysis linked music-based interventions to **lower cortisol, heart rate and blood pressure**.<sup>[<a href="#ref1">1</a>]</sup> A Cochrane review of 81 studies in people with cancer found music interventions **may ease anxiety, depression, pain and fatigue**, with no harm reported.<sup>[<a href="#ref2">2</a>]</sup> Another Cochrane review found music **probably improves sleep quality** in adults with insomnia.<sup>[<a href="#ref3">3</a>]</sup> And a small randomised trial showed singing-bowl sound can trigger an **acute relaxation response**.<sup>[<a href="#ref4">4</a>]</sup>

Notice the careful words the scientists use: "may," "probably," "associated with." That hedging isn't weakness; it's honesty. The certainty of much of this evidence is rated low to moderate because many studies are small. The benefits are real, but modest, and centred on **relaxation and wellbeing**, not on treating disease.

## About those "magic frequency" claims

You may have seen bold statements that a specific frequency (432 Hz or 528 Hz, say) has special healing powers. These make for great marketing and have very little rigorous evidence behind them. What helps you in a session is far more down-to-earth: steady, pleasant sound, a safe space to lie still, slow breathing, and the simple permission to do nothing for an hour. If a claim sounds like a miracle, treat it the way you'd treat any too-good-to-be-true promise.

<div class="callout">A good rule of thumb: trustworthy sound therapy talks about <em>relaxation, stress and comfort</em>, names its limits, and never asks you to stop your medical care. Be cautious of anyone who promises to cure, who discourages seeing a doctor, or who guarantees results.</div>

## Why honesty is the whole point

Holding sound therapy to a fair standard doesn't diminish it; it protects it. The practice doesn't need exaggerated claims to be worthwhile; a reliable, low-risk way to help a stressed body rest is genuinely valuable on its own. That's the version of sound therapy I practise: grounded in what the research actually shows, clear about what it doesn't, and always working alongside your healthcare rather than against it.
