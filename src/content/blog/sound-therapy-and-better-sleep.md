Sleep is the body's most important repair window, yet it's the first thing to suffer when we're stressed. The cruel irony of a tired-but-wired night is that the harder you try to sleep, the more alert you feel. Sound therapy approaches the problem from the side, not head-on: instead of forcing sleep, it helps create the calm conditions in which sleep can arrive on its own.

## Why winding down is a skill, not a switch

Falling asleep isn't a switch you flip; it's a gradual hand-off from your alert "doing" state to your restful "being" state. When the day has kept your nervous system revved up, that hand-off stalls. As I explore in [how sound calms the nervous system](/blog/how-sound-calms-the-nervous-system/), steady, predictable sound gives your attention something soft to settle on, which helps ease the body out of high alert — exactly the shift that precedes sleep.

## What the research says

The most rigorous look at this comes from a Cochrane review by Jespersen and colleagues, updated in 2022, which examined listening to music for adults with insomnia symptoms. It found that music **probably improves subjective sleep quality** compared with no treatment or usual care.<sup>[<a href="#ref1">1</a>]</sup> The authors rate the certainty as **moderate** — promising, but with the honest caveat that participants knew which "treatment" they were receiving, which can colour results.

Why might it help? Part of the answer is relaxation. A 2020 meta-analysis found music-based interventions were associated with lower physiological arousal — reduced cortisol, slower heart rate, lower blood pressure.<sup>[<a href="#ref2">2</a>]</sup> A calmer body at bedtime is simply a body more ready to sleep.

<div class="callout">Sound therapy isn't a sleeping pill and won't override a genuine sleep disorder. Think of it as sleep <em>hygiene</em> — one of several gentle habits, like dimming lights and screens, that tell your body the day is done.</div>

## Using sound to sleep better

A live sound session is a wonderful reset, but you can borrow the principle at home too. Keep the sound **steady and low** rather than dramatic — the goal is monotony, not stimulation. Use it as the **last step** of a wind-down routine, after screens are away and lights are low. And let go of trying: the point isn't to "achieve" sleep, but to relax so thoroughly that you stop noticing when it arrives.

## When to see a professional

If poor sleep persists for weeks, leaves you exhausted during the day, or comes with low mood or anxiety, please speak to your doctor. Insomnia can have underlying causes that deserve proper care, and the gold-standard treatment for chronic insomnia is a structured therapy called CBT-I. Sound can sit alongside that beautifully — but it isn't a substitute for it.
